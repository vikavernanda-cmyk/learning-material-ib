    Topic B.4 · Thermodynamics (HL)

    Thermodynamics

    Every process below starts from the same point on a P–V diagram. Which path the gas takes — constant volume, constant pressure, constant temperature, or no heat at all — decides everything about Q, ΔU and W.

    four paths, one starting point — scroll to see how each one works
    Definitions

    Closed vs. Isolated Systems

    The First Law applies inside a closed system — get this boundary condition right before anything else.

    Closed system

    • No mass transfer in or out
    • Energy can be transferred in both directions — as heat and work

    Isolated system

    • No mass transfer
    • No energy transferred, at all

    From here on, every diagram and formula assumes we're inside a closed system.

    Which system allows energy to move in or out (as heat and work), but never allows mass in or out?

    Core Law

    The First Law: Conservation of Energy

    For a closed system, this is just conservation of energy written for heat and work.

    Q = ΔU + W

    Q, ΔU and W are all measured in joules — heat, change in internal energy, and work, respectively.

    Work done by / on a closed system

    W = P · ΔV

    P in pascals, ΔV (change in volume) in m³, W in joules.

    Work done = the area under a P–V diagram. The direction of the arrow (i.e. whether volume increases or decreases) sets the sign.

    Clausius sign conventions

    • Q = + heat flows from surroundings into the system
    • ΔU = + internal energy increases
    • W = + system does work on the surroundings
    • W = − surroundings do work on the system
    Change in Internal Energy

    ΔU for an Ideal Gas

    ΔU = 32 n R ΔT
    ΔU = 32 N kB ΔT

    where N = n · NA (number of molecules = moles × Avogadro's number)

    Worked Example 1

    Isovolumetric Cooling, A → B

    An ideal gas undergoes an isovolumetric (constant-volume) change from state A to state B.

    Given

    • At A: P = 4×10⁶ Pa, V = 1.5×10⁻⁴ m³, T = 612 K
    • At B: T = 386 K

    Q: How much thermal energy is removed from the gas going from A to B?

    Step 1 — Read the graph

    Isovolumetric ⇒ volume is fixed. Temperature falls, so (from PV = nRT) pressure must fall too — a vertical line straight down from A to B.

    Thermal energy is removed — flowing from the system to the surroundings — so we expect Q negative.

    Step 2 — Apply the First Law

    Q = ΔU + W

    Isovolumetric ⇒ ΔV = 0 ⇒ W = P·ΔV = 0

    so Q = ΔU

    And ΔU = 3/2 · n R · ΔT. From PV = nRT: nR = PAVA / TA

    Q = 3/2 · (PAVA / TA) · ΔT

    Q = 3/2 · [(4×10⁶ × 1.5×10⁻⁴) / 612] × (386 − 612)

    Q = −332 J

    In this example ΔV = 0. Why is W = 0, and why does the negative Q make physical sense?

    Thermodynamic Processes

    Four Named Processes

    Every case below still obeys Q = ΔU + W and PV = nRT — only the constraint changes.

    1

    Isochoric (constant volume)

    PV = nRT, V constant ⇒ P ∝ T. As T↑, P↑.

    CaseΔUWQ
    P↓ (T↓)−0−

    W = P·ΔV = 0 since ΔV = 0 always. If T↓, internal energy falls and heat leaves the system (Q −).

    2

    Isobaric (constant pressure)

    PV = nRT, P constant ⇒ V ∝ T. As T↑, V↑.

    CaseΔUWQ
    V↑ (T↑)+++

    Volume and temperature both rise ⇒ internal energy rises (ΔU +). Gas expands, doing positive work (W = P·ΔV, +). Heat flows in for both effects (Q +).

    3

    Isothermal (constant temperature)

    PV = nRT, T constant ⇒ P ∝ 1/V. As V↑, P↓.

    ΔU: since T is constant, ΔT = 0 ⇒ ΔU = 3/2 nR·ΔT = 0

    CaseΔUWQ
    V↓ (compression, V₂<V₁)0−−

    With ΔU = 0, the First Law gives Q = W directly — so Q takes the same sign as the work done.

    4

    Adiabatic (no thermal energy transfer)

    Q = 0 by definition — the curve is steeper than an isothermal curve on a P–V diagram.

    For this case (gas expands): Q = 0, W = + (W = P·ΔV, expanding). From Q = ΔU + W: 0 = ΔU + (+) ⇒ ΔU = −

    CaseΔUWQ
    Expansion−+0
    PAVA5/3 = PBVB5/3

    holds for an ideal, monatomic gas undergoing an adiabatic process (since PV5/3 = constant).

    For an isobaric expansion (constant pressure, volume increasing), what are the signs of ΔU, W and Q?

    Why Does This Happen?

    Reasoning It Through

    Starting from PV = nRT, for a fixed amount of gas: P ∝ T/V.

    Isothermal

    Temperature is constant, so only the volume effect matters: P ∝ 1/V, i.e. PV = constant.

    Adiabatic

    The gas performs work without receiving heat, so it loses internal energy → temperature decreases.

    • Isothermal: pressure falls because molecules spread through a larger volume (needed to keep pressure consistent with constant T)
    • Adiabatic: pressure falls for two reasons stacked together — the gas occupies more volume and it's colder, so molecules collide with the walls less often. That's why the adiabatic curve drops more steeply than the isothermal one — the gas expands against external pressure while cooling, performing work without receiving heat.
    Worked Example 2

    A Three-Stage Cycle

    A monatomic ideal gas is taken around a cycle: isothermal expansion 2→3, isovolumetric(isochoric) heat extraction 3→1, then adiabatic compression 1→2 back to the start. The volume at 3 (and 1) is twice the volume at 2.

    Given

    • At A: P = 2×10⁶ Pa, T = 306 K, V = 0.75×10⁻⁴ m³
    • VB = 2VA (the isothermal expansion doubles the volume)
    • Work done by the gas during the isothermal expansion A→B = 208 J

    a) Thermal energy supplied during A→B

    Q = ΔU + W

    Isothermal ⇒ ΔT = 0 ⇒ ΔU = 3/2 nR·ΔT = 0

    so Q = W = 208 J

    b) Gas temperature at C

    C connects back to A along the adiabatic leg, so PVᵞ = constant links states A and C directly. The general gas law PV/T = constant links any two states.

    Since B→C is isovolumetric, VC = VB = 2VA.

    PCVC5/3 = PAVA5/3 ⇒ PC = PA · (VA / 2VA)5/3 = PA · (1/2)5/3

    TC = TA · PCVCPAVA = 306 · (1/2)5/3 · 2

    = 306 × 0.314 × 2

    TC ≈ 193 K

    Why can PVᵞ = constant be applied directly between states A and C, even though the gas actually travels A→B→C first?

    Converting Heat to Work

    Heat Engines

    A heat engine performs useful work by taking heat from a hot reservoir, converting part of it into work, and rejecting the rest to a cold reservoir — cycle after cycle.

    Cyclic processes: ΔU = 0

    A heat engine returns to its starting state every cycle, so over one complete loop on a P–V diagram, the net change in internal energy is always zero.

    Efficiency of a heat engine

    η = useful workinput energy = Q_H − Q_CQ_H

    Since ΔU = 0 over a full cycle, Q = W for the cycle as a whole — so the useful work done equals the heat absorbed minus the heat rejected.

    Most Efficient Cyclic Process

    The Carnot Cycle

    The Carnot cycle is the most efficient possible cycle between two reservoirs, built from four reversible stages.

    • A → B isothermal expansion
    • B → C adiabatic expansion
    • C → D isothermal compression
    • D → A adiabatic compression

    Deriving the Carnot efficiency

    η = Q_H − Q_CQ_H = T_H − T_CT_H = T_HT_H − T_CT_H

    η = 1 − T_C / T_H

    Temperatures must be in kelvin — this is a ratio of absolute temperatures, so an absolute scale is required.

    Worked example: power plant efficiency

    A power plant is modelled as a Carnot heat engine. Hot reservoir: 657°C. Cold reservoir: 372°C. What is its efficiency?

    η = 1 − T_C/T_H = 1 − (372 + 273) / (657 + 273)

    η ≈ 0.302 → about 30%

    Why must temperatures in the Carnot efficiency formula be in kelvin, not °C?

    One of The Strangest Quantities

    Entropy

    Entropy is a measure of the amount of disorder of the particles in a system. A warmer object has more entropy (more movement); a colder object has less entropy.

    The 2nd Law of Thermodynamics states that total entropy, or disorder, in an isolated system must always increase over time during any spontaneous process

    Macroscopic definition

    In terms of thermal energy (ΔQ) and temperature (T)

    ΔS = ΔQT

    ΔS is measured in joules per kelvin (J/K). ΔQ is in joules (J), T is in kelvin (K).

    Microscopic definition

    In terms of possible microstates (Ω)

    S = kB · ln Ω

    kB is the Boltzmann constant; Ω is the number of possible microstates the particles could be arranged in.

    Worked example: which point has the largest entropy?

    A gas is taken around the cycle A → B → C → D on a P–V diagram: A→B is an isothermal expansion, B→C is an isovolumetric (constant-volume) process, and C→D is adiabatic. At which point is the entropy largest?

    ΔS = ΔQT → entropy change
    • A → B, isothermal expansion: T is constant, so ΔU = 0, meaning Q = W. Since the gas expands, W is positive, so Q > 0 → ΔS increases from A to B.
    • B → C, isovolumetric: ΔV = 0, so W = 0 and Q = ΔU. Since ΔU = 32nRΔT and PV = nRT, as P falls and T falls, ΔU < 0 → ΔS decreases from B to C.
    • C → D, adiabatic: ΔQ = 0 by definition, so ΔS = 0 — entropy stays constant.

    Entropy is largest at B — it rises all the way from A to B, then falls from B to C, and holds steady from C to D.

    1. In your own words: why is Q negative when a gas releases heat to its surroundings?

    By the Clausius convention, Q is defined as positive when heat flows into the system from the surroundings. If heat is instead leaving the system, that flow is in the opposite direction, so Q is negative.

    2. Why is the adiabatic curve steeper than the isothermal curve on a P–V diagram?

    On the isothermal curve, pressure only falls because the gas spreads into a larger volume. On the adiabatic curve, pressure falls for that same reason, plus the gas is also cooling (since it does work without receiving heat) — colder gas molecules collide with the container walls less often, dropping the pressure further for the same increase in volume.

    3. Starting from Q = ΔU + W, show algebraically why W = 0 in an isochoric process.

    Work is defined as W = P·ΔV. "Isochoric" means constant volume, so ΔV = 0 by definition. Substituting: W = P × 0 = 0. This leaves Q = ΔU — all the heat transferred goes directly into changing internal energy (and hence temperature).

    4. Why does a Carnot engine's efficiency only depend on the reservoir temperatures, not on the gas used?

    Because every stage of the Carnot cycle is reversible, the efficiency reduces to η = 1 − T_C/T_H — a result that comes purely from the ratio of absolute reservoir temperatures. No gas-specific quantity (like n or the type of gas) survives in the final expression.

    Current & Circuits

    Charge moving through a conductor, pushed by a potential difference, resisted by a resistor. From that one idea, every circuit rule below follows.

    current · potential difference · resistance · series & parallel · internal resistance · sensors
    Definitions

    Electric Current

    Electric current is the movement of charge.

    Conventional current vs. electron flow

    • Conventional current is defined as the direction positive charges would flow — from + to − around the external circuit.
    • In reality, it's negatively charged electrons that move, and they travel in the opposite direction — from − to +.
    • Every circuit rule in this topic is written in terms of conventional current, by convention.

    Defining current

    I = ΔqΔt

    q = charge, measured in coulombs (C). t = time, measured in seconds (s). Current I is measured in amperes (A), where 1 A = 1 C/s.

    Conventional current in a circuit flows in the direction that:

    Doing Work on Charge

    Potential Difference

    To move charge around a circuit, work must be done on it — that work, per unit charge, is the potential difference.

    Potential difference (V)

    V = Wq

    W = work done per charge to move a positive charge between two points in a circuit. Unit: volt (V), where 1 V = 1 J/C.

    The electron-volt

    Since work done equals kinetic energy transferred, W = Ek, so V = Ek/q → Ek = qV.

    Ek = q · V  →  for an electron: Ek = e · V, where e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
    1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

    One electron-volt is the kinetic energy gained by a single electron accelerated through a potential difference of 1 V — a convenient energy unit used later for particle and atomic-scale conversions.

    An electron accelerates through a potential difference of 2 V. What kinetic energy does it gain, in eV?

    Core Relationships

    Ohm's Law, Resistivity & Power

    Resistance describes how hard it is to push current through a component. Resistivity describes that same property for the material itself, independent of shape.

    Ohm's Law

    V = IR

    Valid for an ohmic conductor at constant temperature: current is directly proportional to potential difference, giving a straight-line V–I graph through the origin. R is the resistance, measured in ohms (Ω), where 1 Ω = 1 V/A.

    Rewriting as V = RI matches y = mx + b: plotting V (y-axis) against I (x-axis) gives a straight line whose gradient equals R.

    Resistance

    R = VI

    Opposition to current flow. Depends on the component and, for most materials, its temperature.

    Resistivity

    ρ = RAL

    ρ = resistivity (Ω·m), a property of the material only. L = length (m), A = cross-sectional area (m²) = πr² for a wire. Longer wires resist more; thicker wires resist less.

    Exam tip

    • Cross-sectional area for a wire: A = πr² — found on data booklet p.2, always convert diameter to radius first.
    • Units for resistivity: [Ω][m²] / [m] = Ω·m.
    • If a V–I graph is a straight line through the origin → ohmic (constant resistance). If it curves → non-ohmic (variable resistance, e.g. a filament lamp).

    Electrical power

    P = IV = I²R = V²R

    All three forms come from combining P = IV with V = IR. Power is measured in watts (W), where 1 W = 1 J/s.

    Exam tip

    Remember also that Power = work done / time = energy / time. This is especially useful when a question spans two topics — e.g. a motor lifting a mass a height h in time t: P = energy/time = mgh/t.

    Worked example — power dissipated in a mixed circuit

    In the circuit below, a 8.0 V battery drives current through a 2.0 Ω resistor in series with two 4.0 Ω resistors connected in parallel. Find the total power dissipated in the circuit.

    Step 1 — find total equivalent resistance.

    Parallel pair: 1/Rp = 1/4 + 1/4 = 2/4 → Rp = 2.0 Ω

    Add the series resistor: R = Rp + 2.0 = 2.0 + 2.0 = 4.0 Ω

    Step 2 — we know V and R, so use P = V²/R.

    P = 8.0² / 4.0 = 64 / 4

    P = 16 W

    A resistor has V = 6 V across it and I = 2 A through it. What power does it dissipate?

    A wire of resistivity ρ, length L and cross-sectional area A has resistance R. If the wire is stretched so its length doubles (volume, and hence material, stays the same, so A halves), what happens to R?

    Foundations

    Building a Circuit

    A battery supplies the potential difference (work). That work causes charge to move — and moving charge is current.

    Ammeters & voltmeters

    • Ammeter — counts coulombs per second (current). Connected in series with the component being measured.
    • Voltmeter — measures the difference in potential (p.d.) across a component. Connected in parallel across it.

    Ideal ammeter

    R = 0

    All current passes through it without any voltage being dropped across it.

    Ideal voltmeter

    R = ∞

    No current passes through it — it measures p.d. without disturbing the circuit.

    Resistors

    A resistor has resistance R (unit: Ω, ohm) and uses potential difference to resist the current flowing through it.

    Series Circuits

    Series Circuits

    One loop, one path — every component shares the same current.

    Series circuit rules

    • Current — the same everywhere (measured by an ammeter anywhere in the loop): I = I1 = I2 = …
    • Voltage — the battery's p.d. is shared out across each component: V = V1 + V2 + …
    • Total resistance — resistances simply add: Rs = R1 + R2 + …
    Rs = R1 + R2 + …

    Two resistors, 4 Ω and 6 Ω, are connected in series across a 10 V battery. What current flows?

    Parallel Circuits

    Parallel Circuits

    Multiple paths for current, all starting and ending at the same two points — so every branch shares the same p.d.

    Parallel circuit rules

    • Current — splits between branches and recombines: I = I1 + I2 + …
    • Voltage — the same across every branch: V = V1 = V2 = …
    • Total resistance — reciprocals add, so Rp is always smaller than the smallest individual resistor: 1Rp = 1R1 + 1R2 + …

    Two resistors, 4 Ω and 4 Ω, are connected in parallel. What is the total resistance?

    Real Batteries

    Internal Resistance

    Every real battery has some resistance of its own — so not all of its EMF reaches the external circuit.

    EMF and terminal voltage

    The EMF (ε) is the total energy per charge the battery supplies. Some of that is "lost" driving current through the battery's own internal resistance (r), leaving only the terminal voltage available to the external circuit.

    ε = I(R + r) = IR + Ir
    V = ε − Ir

    IR is the terminal p.d. (V) delivered to the external circuit; Ir is the "lost volts" dropped inside the battery itself.

    Opening up the equation

    Starting from ε = IR + Ir and rearranging for IR:

    IR = ε − Ir

    IR — what you actually get: the p.d. across the terminal (V)

    ε — what the battery tries to give (V)

    Ir — what's lost by the internal resistance of the battery (V)

    Exam tip

    • EMF is NOT a force. It is a p.d., measured in volts — the name is historical.
    • EMF is what the battery is trying to put out, but the internal resistance "eats" some of it — like a middle man taking a cut of the profits.

    Worked example — parallel resistors with internal resistance

    A circuit consists of 10 identical 5.0 Ω resistors connected in parallel.

    a) What is the equivalent resistance of the 10 resistors?

    1/Rp = 1/5 + 1/5 + … (10 times) = 10 × (1/5) = 2/1

    Rp = 0.5 Ω

    b) The internal resistance of the battery is 0.1 Ω, and its EMF is 6.0 V. What is the current at the battery?

    ε = I(R + r) → I = ε / (R + r)

    I = 6 / (0.5 + 0.1) = 6 / 0.6

    I = 10 A

    c) What is the potential difference across the terminal?

    V = IR = (10)(0.5)

    V = 5.0 V

    The battery "tries" to give out 6 V at the terminal, but the internal resistance "eats" 1 V, leaving only 5 V for the circuit.

    A battery with EMF 6 V and internal resistance 1 Ω drives a current of 2 A. What is the terminal voltage?

    A battery of EMF 9 V with internal resistance 0.5 Ω is connected to a 4 Ω resistor. What current flows?

    Non-Ohmic Components

    Thermistors & LDRs

    Not every component obeys Ohm's law — some resistors are designed so their resistance responds to the environment, making them useful as sensors.

    Thermistor (NTC)

    • Resistance decreases as temperature increases (a Negative Temperature Coefficient thermistor).
    • Higher temperature frees more charge carriers, so current flows more easily.
    • Used in temperature sensing and thermostats.

    LDR (Light-Dependent Resistor)

    • Resistance decreases as light intensity increases.
    • More incident light frees more charge carriers in the semiconductor material.
    • Used in light sensing, e.g. automatic street lights.

    Variable Voltage Dividers

    Potentiometers

    A potentiometer is a three-terminal resistor with a sliding contact (wiper), used to output a variable, adjustable potential difference.

    How it works

    • The full supply p.d. is connected across the two end terminals of a resistive track.
    • The wiper taps off a fraction of that p.d., set by its position along the track — moving the wiper smoothly varies the output voltage from 0 up to the full supply voltage.
    • Because it's a voltage divider rather than a single variable resistor in series, it can supply the full range of output voltages, including right down to 0 V.

    Common uses

    Volume controls, providing a known reference/comparison voltage in measuring circuits, and calibrating other sensors.

    Thermal Energy Transfers

    From vibrating particles, to the heating curve of melting ice, to the glow of a black body — how thermal energy moves, and what temperature actually measures.

    Foundations

    States of Matter, Celsius & Kelvin

    Particles are never still — even in a solid they constantly vibrate. What changes between states is how far particles can move, and how tightly they're held together.

    Solid

    Fixed shape, fixed volume — particles vibrate about fixed positions, held tightly by strong forces.

    Liquid

    No fixed shape, fixed volume — particles can slide past one another but stay close together.

    Gas

    No fixed shape, no fixed volume — particles move freely and independently, far apart from one another.

    Celsius & Kelvin

    Reference point°CK
    Freezing point of water0 °C273 K
    Boiling point of water100 °C373 K
    Absolute zero−273 °C0 K

    The Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero — the coldest temperature theoretically possible, where particle motion is at its minimum. Because it has no negative values, Kelvin is the scale physics equations use.

    T (K) = T (°C) + 273
    Kinetic Model

    What Temperature Really Measures

    Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of a substance's particles — nothing else.

    ⟨Ek⟩ = 32 kB T

    where kB is the Boltzmann constant (1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹). Raising the temperature raises the average kinetic energy — and particles move faster.

    Cold

    Low average kinetic energy — particles move slowly.

    Hot

    High average kinetic energy — particles move quickly. But because Ek = ½mv², doubling T only multiplies speed by √2, not 2.

    Check — Concept + calculation

    (a) A gas is heated from 300 K to 600 K. Explain why the particles are not moving exactly twice as fast, even though their average kinetic energy has exactly doubled.

    (b) Calculate the average kinetic energy of a single gas particle at 310 K.

    (a) ⟨Ek⟩ ∝ T, so doubling T doubles ⟨Ek⟩. But Ek = ½mv², so v ∝ √T — speed only increases by a factor of √2 ≈ 1.41.

    (b) ⟨Ek⟩ = (3/2)kBT = (3/2)(1.38×10⁻²³)(310) ≈ 6.42 × 10⁻²¹ J

    Energy Transfer

    Heat & Internal Energy

    Heat (Q)

    Energy transferred between a system and its surroundings because of a temperature difference. It's measured in joules, and only exists while the transfer is happening — a substance doesn't "contain" heat. Heat always flows from hot to cold — energy naturally spreads from where particles move faster to where they move slower, never the reverse, unless work is done to force it.

    Internal energy (U)

    The total energy stored inside a substance — the sum of every particle's kinetic energy and every particle's potential energy.

    U = ΣEk + ΣEp

    Kinetic part

    Energy of particle motion — this is the part temperature tracks.

    Potential part

    Energy stored in the bonds and forces between particles — depends on how far apart particles are, not how fast they move.

    Check — Concept

    Match each term to its correct description: Temperature, Heat, Internal energy.

    • 1. The total kinetic + potential energy of all particles in a substance.
    • 2. A measure of the average kinetic energy of particles.
    • 3. Energy transferred between a system and surroundings due to a temperature difference.

    1 → Internal energy   2 → Temperature   3 → Heat

    A common exam trap: only heat is "energy in motion" between two things — temperature and internal energy are both properties a substance simply has.

    Energy Transfer

    Specific Heat Capacity

    Adding heat to a substance speeds its particles up — which is exactly what raises its temperature.

    Definition

    Specific heat capacity, c, is the energy needed to raise the temperature of one unit of mass of a substance by 1 Kelvin.

    Q = mcΔT

    c in J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. A high c (like water) means a substance resists changing temperature; a low c means it heats up and cools down quickly.

    Check — Exam-style

    Calculate the heat energy needed to raise the temperature of 2.0 kg of water from 15 °C to 95 °C. c(water) = 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹

    Q = mcΔT = (2.0)(4200)(95 − 15) = (2.0)(4200)(80)

    Q = 672,000 J = 6.72 × 10⁵ J

    Energy Transfer

    Specific Latent Heat

    During a change of phase, heat is no longer used to speed particles up — it's used to break or form the bonds between them, so temperature stops rising even while heat keeps flowing in.

    Why does temperature pause?

    All the incoming energy goes into pulling particles apart (or letting them come together) rather than increasing their kinetic energy — so average KE, and therefore temperature, stays constant until the phase change is complete.

    Melting: solid → liquid  ·  Freezing: liquid → solid

    Boiling: liquid → gas  ·  Condensing: gas → liquid

    Definition

    Specific latent heat, L, is the energy per unit mass absorbed or released during a phase change (at constant temperature).

    Q = mL

    L in J kg⁻¹. Latent heat of fusion — melting/freezing. Latent heat of vaporization — boiling/condensing (always the larger value, since particles must fully separate).

    Check — Exam-style

    Calculate the energy needed to melt 0.50 kg of ice at 0 °C. L(fusion, water) = 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹

    Q = mL = (0.50)(3.34×10⁵)

    Q = 1.67 × 10⁵ J

    Putting It Together

    The Heating Curve

    Plotting temperature against heat added produces a curve with two kinds of segment — and each is governed by a different equation.

    Sloped section

    A single phase is warming or cooling — governed by Q = mcΔT.

    Flat section

    A phase change is in progress — temperature holds steady while Q = mL is absorbed or released.

    Check — Exam-style · full heating curve

    Calculate the total energy needed to convert 0.20 kg of ice at −10 °C completely into water vapor at 100 °C.

    c(ice)=2100 J kg⁻¹K⁻¹ · L(fusion)=3.34×10⁵ J kg⁻¹ · c(water)=4200 J kg⁻¹K⁻¹ · L(vaporization)=2.26×10⁶ J kg⁻¹

    Warm ice −10→0 °C: Q₁ = mcΔT = (0.20)(2100)(10) = 4200 J

    Melt ice at 0 °C: Q₂ = mL = (0.20)(3.34×10⁵) = 66,800 J

    Warm water 0→100 °C: Q₃ = mcΔT = (0.20)(4200)(100) = 84,000 J

    Boil water at 100 °C: Q₄ = mL = (0.20)(2.26×10⁶) = 452,000 J

    Qtotal = Q₁+Q₂+Q₃+Q₄ ≈ 6.07 × 10⁵ J (607 kJ)

    Thermal Energy Transfer

    Conduction, Convection & Radiation

    Heat always moves from hot to cold — but it can travel by three completely different routes.

    1

    Conduction

    Transfer of thermal energy through a material — or between two materials in contact — without any bulk movement of the material itself. Fast-vibrating particles collide with slower neighbours and pass kinetic energy along, particle by particle. Most effective in solids, especially metals, where free electrons carry energy very quickly.

    Q/t = kAΔTd

    k = thermal conductivity (W m⁻¹K⁻¹) · A = cross-sectional area (m²) · ΔT = temperature difference (K) · d = thickness (m). A larger k or A, or bigger ΔT, speeds up conduction; a thicker material slows it down.

    Example: a metal spoon left in hot soup soon becomes hot to touch. A woollen sweater keeps you warm largely because still air trapped between fibres has very low k, so almost no conduction escapes.

    2

    Convection

    Transfer of thermal energy through the actual bulk movement of a fluid (liquid or gas). Heated fluid expands, becomes less dense, and rises; cooler, denser fluid sinks to take its place — setting up a convection current. Because it needs particles free to flow, convection only happens in fluids, never solids.

    Natural convection

    Driven purely by density differences — e.g. a room radiator warms nearby air, which rises and circulates on its own.

    Forced convection

    A fan or pump actively moves the fluid — e.g. a car's radiator fan, or a central heating pump.

    Example: a sea breeze forms because land heats up faster than the sea in daytime; warm air rises over land and cooler sea air flows in to replace it.

    3

    Radiation

    Transfer of energy by electromagnetic waves (mostly infrared). Needs no medium — it travels through a vacuum just fine, which is how the Sun's energy reaches Earth. Every object above absolute zero radiates; hotter objects radiate more energy, at shorter wavelengths.

    Emit

    An object radiates electromagnetic waves outward. Hotter, darker, rougher (matte) surfaces are better emitters.

    Absorb

    An object absorbs incoming radiation, converting it into internal energy. Dark, matte surfaces absorb well; light, shiny surfaces mostly reflect.

    A surface that is a good emitter at a given wavelength is also a good absorber at that wavelength (Kirchhoff's law) — matte black surfaces both heat up fastest in the sun and cool down fastest at night.

    Comparing the three mechanisms

    MechanismNeedsWhere it dominates
    ConductionMatter in contactSolids, especially metals
    ConvectionA fluidLiquids and gases
    RadiationNo medium at allWorks through a vacuum

    Check — Concept + application

    (a) Explain why radiation is the only one of the three transfer mechanisms that can bring energy from the Sun to the Earth.

    (b) A cooking pot has a metal base, sits over a gas flame, and has boiling water inside with steam rising above it. Identify which transfer mechanism is mainly responsible at each of: (i) flame → pot base, (ii) pot base → water, (iii) water → rising steam.

    (a) The space between the Sun and Earth is a vacuum, so conduction and convection (which both need matter) are impossible — only radiation can cross empty space.

    (b) (i) Radiation (and some conduction from direct flame contact) heats the metal base. (ii) Conduction through the solid metal base into the water. (iii) Convection — heated water rises, carrying energy with it.

    Black-Body Radiation

    The Black-Body Spectrum

    An idealised perfect absorber turns out to be the key to understanding lightbulbs, stars, and — eventually — the Earth's own temperature.

    What is a black body?

    An idealised object that absorbs all electromagnetic radiation falling on it, at every wavelength, reflecting none. Because it wastes nothing on reflection, when heated a black body also emits the maximum possible amount of thermal radiation for its temperature — it has emissivity ε = 1.

    No real object is a perfect black body, but many everyday sources come close enough to be modelled as one. Example: an electric current heating a tungsten filament to ~2500–3000 K makes it glow with a continuous spectrum resembling black-body radiation — mostly invisible infrared, which is why incandescent bulbs are so energy-inefficient compared with LEDs.

    The black-body spectrum

    A black body doesn't emit at one single wavelength — it emits a continuous range, with intensity at each wavelength described by a curve that depends only on temperature.

    As T increases: total area under the curve grows (more power), and the peak shifts to shorter wavelength.

    Wien's displacement law

    The peak wavelength is inversely proportional to absolute temperature.

    λmax · T = b

    where b = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K. Hotter objects glow bluer-white (peak shifts to shorter wavelengths); cooler stars glow red. The human body (~310 K) peaks deep in the infrared — invisible to our eyes.

    The inverse square law

    As radiation spreads from a small source, its power is shared over the surface of an ever-expanding sphere. Since sphere surface area grows with r², intensity falls off as 1/r².

    I = P4πr²

    Example: Mars orbits roughly 1.5× further from the Sun than Earth — so it receives only about (1/1.5)² ≈ 44% of the solar intensity Earth gets.

    Power radiated by a black body — Stefan–Boltzmann law

    P = σAT⁴

    For a real (non-ideal) emitter with emissivity ε: P = εσAT⁴, where σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻²K⁻⁴. Because power scales with the fourth power of temperature, even a small rise in T causes a large rise in radiated power — doubling absolute temperature multiplies radiated power by 2⁴ = 16.

    Check — Exam-style

    A tungsten filament of surface area 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ m² and emissivity 0.35 operates at 2700 K. Calculate the power it radiates.

    P = εσAT⁴ = (0.35)(5.67×10⁻⁸)(6.0×10⁻⁵)(2700)⁴

    (2700)⁴ ≈ 5.31×10¹¹

    P ≈ 63 W

    The Greenhouse Effect

    Black bodies, the inverse square law, and T⁴ come together to explain why the Earth is a habitable temperature — and why that's changing.

    Solar Radiation

    The Sun's Radiation Spectrum

    The Sun's photosphere is close to a black body at roughly 5800 K, so — by Wien's law — its spectrum peaks in the visible range, spanning from short, energetic ultraviolet down through visible light to infrared.

    Ultraviolet (~5%)

    Shortest wavelength, highest energy per photon; interacts with the ozone layer, causes sunburn.

    Visible light (~43%)

    ~400–700 nm; the range human eyes detect, and where the Sun's output peaks.

    Infrared radiation (~52%)

    Longest wavelength; felt as warmth, and the wavelength range the (much cooler) Earth itself re-emits at.

    Solar Radiation

    Solar Power, Intensity & the Solar Constant

    Treating the Sun as a black body, its total power output follows the Stefan–Boltzmann law.

    P☉ = σA☉T☉⁴

    That power then spreads out through space and, by the inverse square law, its intensity falls with distance. At Earth's mean orbital distance (d ≈ 1.5×10¹¹ m):

    I = P☉4πd²

    The solar constant, S

    The average intensity of solar radiation on a surface held perpendicular to the Sun's rays, just outside Earth's atmosphere: S ≈ 1361 W m⁻².

    Earth's Radiation Budget

    Albedo & Emissivity

    Not all the radiation striking Earth gets absorbed, and Earth itself doesn't radiate exactly like a perfect black body either.

    Albedo, α

    The fraction of incoming radiation reflected without being absorbed. α = 0 means everything is absorbed (perfectly black); α = 1 means everything is reflected. Earth's average albedo is about 0.3 — clouds, ice, oceans, and land each reflect differently.

    Emissivity, ε

    The ratio of radiation a real surface emits compared with what an ideal black body at the same temperature would emit. A perfect black body has ε = 1; a perfect reflector has ε ≈ 0. Real surfaces, including Earth's, sit in between.

    Energy Balance

    Earth Without an Atmosphere

    In equilibrium, the power Earth absorbs must equal the power it radiates away.

    Power absorbed

    Earth intercepts sunlight over its circular cross-section (area πr²), and absorbs the fraction not reflected:

    Pin = Sπr²(1 − α)

    Power emitted

    Earth radiates from its whole spherical surface (area 4πr²), modelled as a black body:

    Pout = σ · 4πr² · T⁴

    Setting Pin = Pout and solving for T gives Earth's equilibrium temperature with no atmosphere — about 255 K (−18 °C), well below the observed average surface temperature.

    Check — Derivation + calculation

    Using S = 1361 W m⁻² and α = 0.30, show that Earth's equilibrium temperature without an atmosphere is approximately 255 K.

    Set Sπr²(1 − α) = σ·4πr²·T⁴

    The πr² cancels partially: S(1 − α) = 4σT⁴

    T⁴ = S(1 − α) / 4σ = (1361)(0.70) / [4(5.67×10⁻⁸)] ≈ 4.20×10⁹

    T ≈ 255 K (−18 °C)

    Energy Balance

    Earth With an Atmosphere

    The atmosphere is largely transparent to incoming shortwave visible sunlight, which passes through and warms the surface. But because Earth is much cooler than the Sun, by Wien's law it re-emits energy as longwave infrared — and greenhouse gases (CO₂, water vapour, methane, etc.) absorb much of that outgoing infrared, then re-emit it in all directions, including back down toward the surface.

    This natural process — the greenhouse effect — lifts Earth's average surface temperature from the airless equilibrium of ~255 K up to the observed ~288 K (15 °C). Without it, Earth's surface would sit well below freezing.

    Climate Change

    What Can Cause Global Warming?

    Any change that shifts the balance between Pin and Pout forces the system to settle at a new, warmer equilibrium temperature.

    Increased solar constant

    More incoming power (Pin ↑) directly, before anything else changes.

    Reduced albedo

    Less radiation reflected, more absorbed — e.g. melting ice exposes darker ocean or land beneath.

    Reduced emissivity

    The surface radiates away less efficiently for its temperature, so energy accumulates.

    Enhanced greenhouse effect

    More greenhouse gases absorb and return more outgoing infrared to the surface, reducing net Pout.

    Check — Applied reasoning

    Using the energy-balance equation above, explain how (a) increasing use of renewable/nuclear energy and (b) reducing albedo loss from melting ice would each affect Earth's equilibrium temperature — one is a mitigation, one is a feedback that worsens warming.

    (a) Renewables/nuclear reduce enhanced-greenhouse gas emissions, keeping outgoing longwave radiation closer to its natural value — helping Pout stay high enough to balance Pin at a lower T.

    (b) Melting ice lowers albedo (α ↓), which increases Pin = Sπr²(1 − α) — more energy absorbed, pushing equilibrium T higher, which melts more ice: a reinforcing (positive) feedback loop.

    Climate Change

    Reducing the Human Contribution

    Greater efficiency

    Less fuel burned per unit of useful energy delivered.

    Coal/oil → natural gas

    Lower CO₂ released per unit of energy generated.

    Combined heat & power

    Waste heat from generation is captured and used, rather than released.

    Renewables & nuclear

    Little to no CO₂ emitted during electricity generation itself.

    Electric vehicles

    Shifts transport emissions away from direct fossil-fuel combustion, especially with low-carbon electricity.

    Carbon capture & storage

    Removes CO₂ from emissions or the atmosphere and stores it, preventing further enhancement of the greenhouse effect.

    The Gas Laws

    Zooming back down to individual particles — how pressure, volume, and temperature of a gas are all connected.

    Foundations

    Avogadro's Hypothesis, the Mole & Avogadro's Constant

    Avogadro's hypothesis

    Equal volumes of different (ideal) gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of particles — regardless of what the gas actually is.

    The mole (mol)

    The SI unit for "amount of substance." One mole of anything contains exactly Avogadro's number of particles.

    NA = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹

    The number of particles N in n moles is simply N = n·NA.

    Foundations

    The Ideal Gas & Defining Its State

    Ideal gas assumptions

    Particles have negligible volume compared with the container; there are no forces between particles except during brief, elastic collisions; particles move in random straight lines between collisions; there is a very large number of particles with a range of speeds.

    The complete state of a fixed amount of gas is described by three macroscopic variables:

    Volume, V

    The space (m³) the gas occupies.

    Temperature, T

    Related to the average kinetic energy (K) of the particles.

    Pressure, p

    Force per unit area (Pa) from particle collisions on the container walls.

    The gas laws that follow each describe how two of these relate when the third is held constant.

    Gas Laws

    Boyle–Mariotte Law

    At constant temperature, the pressure of a fixed mass of gas is inversely proportional to its volume.

    pV = constant  ⇒  p₁V₁ = p₂V₂

    Example

    Pushing down a bicycle pump's piston (assuming temperature stays roughly constant) reduces the trapped air's volume and raises its pressure.

    Gas Laws

    Gay-Lussac's Law

    At constant volume, the pressure of a fixed mass of gas is directly proportional to its absolute (Kelvin) temperature.

    pT = constant  ⇒  p₁T₁ = p₂T₂

    Example

    A sealed rigid gas canister left in a hot car will see its internal pressure rise as temperature rises — the volume can't change.

    Gas Laws

    Charles' Law

    At constant pressure, the volume of a fixed mass of gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature.

    VT = constant  ⇒  V₁T₁ = V₂T₂

    Example

    A balloon left in the sun expands as the air inside warms, since atmospheric pressure outside stays roughly constant.

    Check — Exam-style

    A gas occupies 2.0 L at 300 K and 1.0 atm. It is heated at constant pressure until its volume is 3.0 L. Find the final temperature.

    Charles' law: V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂

    T₂ = T₁ × (V₂/V₁) = 300 × (3.0/2.0)

    T₂ = 450 K

    Combining the Laws

    The Ideal Gas Equation

    Combining Boyle's, Gay-Lussac's, and Charles' laws with Avogadro's hypothesis gives one single equation linking all the state variables at once.

    pV = nRT

    where R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹K⁻¹ is the universal gas constant. In terms of the actual number of particles:

    pV = NkT

    where N is the number of particles and k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ is the Boltzmann constant, related by R = NA·k.

    Physical picture

    p comes from constant particle collisions with the walls; V is the space those particles move in; T tracks the particles' average kinetic energy; n (or N) is simply how many particles are colliding.

    Combining the Laws

    Internal Energy of an Ideal Gas

    An ideal gas has no forces between particles except during collisions, so it stores no intermolecular potential energy — its internal energy is purely kinetic.

    U = 32 nRT

    This follows directly from the average kinetic energy per particle, ⟨Ek⟩ = (3/2)kBT, multiplied by the number of particles. Because internal energy depends only on T, for an ideal gas it is completely independent of pressure or volume individually.

    Check — Exam-style

    Calculate the change in internal energy of 0.40 mol of a monatomic ideal gas when its temperature rises by 60 K.

    ΔU = (3/2)nRΔT = (3/2)(0.40)(8.314)(60)

    ΔU ≈ 300 J
